Can we count solutions faster than brute force? The claim against ETH

Claimed result, under review

The one-paragraph version

Many problems ask whether a set of yes/no switches can satisfy a list of rules. Counting how many settings work is harder still. For about 27 years, researchers have assumed that you cannot beat brute force by more than a fixed exponential rate. In October 2026, OpenAI posted a paper claiming an algorithm that counts all solutions of such problems in subexponential time, using only polynomial memory. If the proof holds, that assumption, the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH), is false.

What ETH says

There is some fixed constant \(c > 0\) such that 3-SAT on \(n\) variables needs time \(2^{cn}\) in the worst case. In plain words: there is no way to beat brute force by a rate better than some fixed exponential.

What the paper claims

Counting all satisfying assignments of any \(k\)-CNF formula can be done deterministically in time \[ 2^{\,O\left(\frac{n \log\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\right)} \] This exponent is \(o(n)\), so it beats \(2^{cn}\) for every \(c > 0\). That is what "subexponential" means here.

Analogy: the combination lock

Picture a lock with \(n\) switches and \(m\) rules, such as "switch 1 OR switch 3 must be on, AND switch 2 must be off." Trying every setting with 100 switches means \(2^{100} \approx 1.3 \times 10^{30}\) checks. At a billion checks per second, that takes about 40 trillion years, roughly 3,000 times the age of the universe. ETH says that for some hard problems, no method can avoid this kind of blow-up. The paper says it can, at least for counting.

Glossary

Variable \(x_i\)

A yes/no switch with value 0 or 1 (false or true).

Literal

A variable or its negation: \(x\) or \(\neg x\).

Clause

An OR of literals, such as \(x_1 \lor \neg x_3 \lor x_7\). At least one must be true.

\(k\)-CNF

An AND of clauses, each with at most \(k\) literals. Every clause must be satisfied.

3-SAT

Deciding whether a 3-CNF formula has any satisfying assignment.

#F (counting)

The number of assignments that satisfy the formula. Counting is harder than deciding.

Subexponential \(2^{o(n)}\)

The exponent grows more slowly than \(n\), so the bound beats \(2^{cn}\) for every fixed \(c > 0\).

Polynomial space

Memory grows only like a polynomial in \(n\) and \(m\), so the computer does not run out of RAM.

Deterministic

The algorithm uses no randomness, so every run gives the same answer.

Status: This is a claim, not an accepted result. OpenAI reports that the proof was checked in Lean, a computer proof assistant. Independent experts were still reviewing it when this page was written. Anthropic has posted a related paper. Say "OpenAI claims" when you present it.

Why not just a faster brute force?

Approach How the time grows
Brute force: try every setting Doubles with every extra switch
The old belief (ETH) Grows at least as fast as some fixed exponential
The new claim Grows much slower than any fixed exponential

The paper gives two versions of this bound. The one shown in its main theorem is the sharper one. The weaker one is the one the reported Lean check covers. Always say which one you mean.

Mistake 1

"So P = NP?"

No. The bound is for counting \(k\)-CNF formulas. Connecting other NP problems to these formulas uses polynomial-time reductions, and those can enlarge the instance. A bound of \(2^{o(\text{size})}\) on the enlarged instance does not give \(2^{o(n)}\) on the original problem. P versus NP is still open.

Mistake 2

"Does the memory blow up?"

No. The algorithm processes groups one at a time, sequentially, and reuses space. The claimed memory use is polynomial in \(n\) and \(m\), not exponential.

Mistake 3

"Does it only check if one solution exists?"

No. It counts every satisfying assignment. A count above zero also answers the yes/no question, so counting is the harder task.

What is claimed

  • Exact counting of satisfying assignments for every fixed \(k \ge 2\).
  • Deterministic, with no randomness.
  • Subexponential time, \(2^{o(n)}\).
  • Polynomial space.
  • Consequence: ETH, SETH, Gap-ETH, and NSETH are all refuted, per the paper.

What is not claimed

  • That P = NP, or that every NP problem is subexponential.
  • That the algorithm is fast in practice. Subexponential can still be astronomically slow for realistic \(n\).
  • That the result has been independently verified. Expert review was still in progress.

Questions you might be asked

Open each one to see a short answer. Try answering before you open it.

Q1Why introduce a parameter \(T\) instead of testing the variables directly?

Testing directly means handling all \(n\) variables at once, which costs about \(2^n\), because the clauses link the variables together. The parameter \(T\) lets us evaluate a related problem at sample points \(T = 1, \dots, Q\), where the groups separate. Since the answer is determined by a polynomial of bounded degree, the values at those sample points are enough to recover the true count at \(T = 0\).

Q2How many groups are there, and how big is each?

The number of groups is \(r \approx \sqrt{\log n}\). Each group has at most \(2n/r\) variables, which is roughly \(n/\sqrt{\log n}\). Careful: \(\sqrt{\log n}\) is the number of groups, not the size of each group. Enumerating one group costs about \(2^{2n/r}\), and that small exponent is what makes the total subexponential.

Q3Are the groups processed in parallel?

No. The groups and sample points are processed one at a time, sequentially. This is why the memory stays polynomial. Processing in parallel would not be what the paper describes.

Q4What is the role of XOR and parity?

The paper writes a negated literal using XOR: a literal is \(x \oplus b\), where \(b\) is a bit. That is just notation for negation. The core counting does not turn OR into XOR. Each clause becomes a polynomial, \(\Phi_e = 1 - \prod(1 - L)\), which equals 1 when the clause is satisfied. Parity reasoning appears in the technical sections, not in the main idea.

Q5Does this kill other hypotheses too?

The paper says its theorem refutes ETH, SETH, Gap-ETH, and NSETH. Results that assume these hypotheses lose their basis if the proof holds. Anthropic reports that its literature search found more than 800 papers with results that depend on ETH or a stronger assumption. Treat that count as their claim.

Q6Has this been verified?

OpenAI reports a Lean verification and an expert-led review. At the time of writing, independent researchers, including members of the group that proposed ETH, were only starting to examine it. Reports also say the Lean file covers a slightly weaker bound than the headline. So the honest answer is: claimed, computer-checked according to the lab, and under expert review.

Q7Is the algorithm practical?

Not necessarily. Subexponential means the exponent is \(o(n)\), but for realistic \(n\), the running time can still be enormous. The paper does not claim the algorithm is fast in practice.

Key takeaways

  1. The claim: exact counting of \(k\)-CNF solutions can be done in \(2^{o(n)}\) time, deterministically.
  2. The idea: write counting as a sum of products of clause polynomials, split variables into about \(\sqrt{\log n}\) groups, and use sample points plus interpolation to handle the links between groups.
  3. The memory: polynomial, because groups are handled one at a time.
  4. The limits: P versus NP is still open, the algorithm is not claimed to be practical, and the result is not yet independently verified.
